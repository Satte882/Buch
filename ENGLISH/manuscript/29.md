## 29

The narrow review left twenty-seven names.

Too many for certainty.

Too few to ignore the gap.

The number had been on the screen since Jonas removed the last duplicates.

27.

No traffic light. No risk scores. No ranking.

Just people inside the defined project scope who, under the agreed criteria, could even fall within the wider review.

Berg stood in front of the screen.

"We built the project structure for exactly this."

Lena sat at the table with both hands flat beside her laptop.

"To combine existing case-relevant information faster."

"Yes."

"Not to turn twenty-seven people into precautionary suspects."

"No one's doing that."

"Not yet."

Daniel looked at the time.

The overnight review had neither cleared the new delivery authorization nor made it look more suspicious.

The replacement provider was real. The unclear mobile number had turned out to be an old data error. Two drivers had been added and were plausible. Some of the access roles had been explained.

A gap still remained.

Several people could touch relevant information or access during the remaining period, and the narrow review could not fully cover their contact and timing overlaps.

The warning's time window had not been confirmed.

Neither had the possible scale of harm.

The genuine new delivery authorization had.

Daniel had barely slept.

Shortly after midnight, Jana had texted:

**Here. Everything quiet.**

No address.

He had read the message and turned the phone face down.

Now it lay beside his notepad with the screen down.

"Criteria," Daniel said.

Jonas put the list on the large monitor.

"Only people within the defined project scope," he said. "Access to sensitive case information or relevant event access. Plus unexplained contact or timing overlaps inside the defined windows."

"No automatic expansion to their contacts?" Daniel asked.

"No."

"No assessment based on a single time overlap alone?"

"No."

"No action from a hit without human review."

Jonas looked at him. "Obviously."

"Say it anyway."

"No action from a hit without human review."

Lena watched Daniel.

"Broader than yesterday," she said.

"Yes."

"And Weber?"

Daniel looked at her.

The name sat between them even though it was not on the screen.

"Exactly why we need alternative explanations."

"Not a reason to bring in even more people."

Berg said, "And the storage unit?"

Lena turned toward him.

"Exactly why the question is hard."

Berg nodded.

No triumph.

Daniel almost liked him for it in that moment.

"Without the wider review group, we probably wouldn't have connected the temporary driver in time," Berg said. "With the same review group, we weighted Weber wrongly."

"Yes," Lena said.

"Then we don't have a clean example for or against it."

"Correct."

Daniel looked at the 27.

That was worse than a clear failure.

He wrote Weber and the storage unit side by side. Under one: misattribution, employer response, downstream effect. Under the other: real threat strand, probably visible earlier because of the wider review.

No success or failure label.

"Now the decision," Lena said.

Daniel looked at both columns.

Neither was empty.

He did not authorize the twenty-seven-person group because the method had proved itself.

He authorized it despite already knowing that both success and harm to an uninvolved person were part of what the method could produce.

That was a different step.

And he knew it at the moment he took it.

A failure would have pointed in one direction.

Instead, the method had simultaneously exposed a real threat strand and harmed an uninvolved person.

"What remains untouched if we stay narrow?" Daniel asked.

Jonas opened the gap list.

Six people with sensitive information access whose relevant contact times had so far been only partly reviewed. Nine people with event access and at least one unexplained overlap. The rest were roles where data was ambiguous or responses still incomplete.

"And if we review broadly?"

"Then we get more explanation work," Jonas said. "Probably a lot of garbage."

"And maybe a hit," Berg said.

Lena looked at Daniel.

"And maybe another Weber."

Daniel nodded.

"Yes."

She apparently waited for more.

Daniel did not give it to her.

He opened the project note containing his return boundary.

**No automatic continuation or expansion. Continuation only after renewed documented justification and authorization.**

He read the sentence silently.

The acute basis had not disappeared. But this specific wider review was exactly the kind of expansion he did not want to become habitual.

He opened a new decision note.

**Basis:** confirmed new delivery authorization; remaining unexplained access/information gap; narrow setup window; real threat strand in the same complex previously identified only through wider connection review.

Then underneath:

**Countervailing risk:** Weber misattribution; correlation does not establish individual involvement; additional people will be drawn into review.

Lena read along.

"And Jana?" she asked.

Daniel looked at her.

"She's not in there."

"Good."

"She's not evidence."

"I know."

The sentence irritated him less than it had the day before.

Maybe because by now he needed it himself.

Berg sat down.

"Decision?"

Daniel looked at the 27 again.

"We review the defined group."

Lena raised her eyes.

"Completely?"

"Under the criteria. No expansion beyond them."

"And what do we do with hits?"

"Explain first. Decide afterward."

She shook her head once.

"You thought that with Weber too."

"Yes."

Daniel said it without defending himself.

"And I'm authorizing it anyway."

The room was briefly quiet.

Then Jonas released the review.

The first results were boring.

A lawyer had five calls with a state office because jurisdiction had been entered incorrectly.

A technician appeared in nighttime access lists because his account was being used for an automated import.

Two phone numbers now belonged to different people.

One employee had called the same internal office during three relevant time windows. Her father was in the hospital there; the extension had been categorized incorrectly.

One access event looked like a nighttime login and was delayed synchronization.

Jonas swore quietly about duplicates.

"So far, only work," Berg said.

"Good," Lena said.

Daniel looked at the explanations being added beside each hit.

People still made the decisions. They read, called, compared, crossed things out again.

That was exactly why it took time.

After forty minutes, seven hits had been explained.

After an hour, eleven.

Three remained open because responses were missing.

Then Jonas said, "Wait."

One word.

The room changed anyway.

Daniel stepped behind him.

A name appeared in three time windows.

Dr. Lena Vogt.

Her name was there because of repeated contacts with an internal data-protection and oversight unit that were not explained in the project documents, not because of her normal case work.

The times were strikingly close to Daniel's exceptional decisions.

And to two phases in which the source later knew information that had not been public.

Jonas said nothing else.

Berg moved closer.

"What is that?"

Lena looked at the screen.

Her face did not change.

"Contacts."

"I can see that."

"Then you know as much as I can tell you about them right now."

Berg turned toward her.

"Seriously?"

"Yes."

Daniel immediately felt his mind building counterhypotheses.

Oversight questions.

Legal consultation.

Documentation.

A complaint about his first access.

A professional review that had to remain confidential.

Something completely ordinary that only looked wrong because of the time windows.

The possibilities came fast.

Too fast.

He had known them with Weber too.

Shared work phone.

Shift swap.

Wrong access assignment.

He had written them down and then weighted them less.

Now he wanted to test every harmless explanation first.

Daniel noticed the difference in himself before anyone said anything.

Lena noticed that he noticed.

"Say it," she said.

"What?"

"That you're looking for reasons why it's different with me."

Daniel looked at the three time windows.

"I'm looking for alternative explanations."

"Faster than you did with Weber."

"Because I have more context about you."

"Exactly."

The sentence was not mocking.

That made it worse.

Daniel knew her work. Her attitude. Her history in the case. He knew how often she had slowed him down when an easy explanation looked too neat.

All of that was context.

All of it was also personal proximity.

Berg said, "Until we've clarified this, you lose access to the source file and the most sensitive project sections."

Jonas turned halfway toward him.

"Completely?"

"The sensitive parts."

"She has active reviews."

"Then hand them over."

Lena's eyes went to Daniel, not Berg.

Daniel could have objected.

The anomaly was not proof.

The anomaly with Weber had not been proof either.

"Which parts exactly?" Daniel asked.

Berg named the source file, provenance review, and the sensitive project information not widely distributed.

"General operational picture?"

"Only as needed for handover until the review is complete."

Lena said, "If you need the rule, don't make it smaller because you know my name."

Daniel looked at her.

"You could explain what the contacts are."

"Formally."

"That takes time."

"Then it takes time."

"You know what's happening tomorrow and you're still giving me nothing?"

Lena leaned back.

"I'm giving you exactly what I can give you outside the proper channel about a confidential oversight contact: that it exists and can be formally explained."

"That's very little."

"For your personal comfort, yes."

Daniel felt anger.

Jonas looked away.

Berg said, "Daniel."

The decision was already on the table in substance.

Daniel opened the note in which he had documented the criteria for the twenty-seven-person group.

No personal exception.

No clause for people he trusted.

He placed the cursor beneath the hit assessment.

**Temporary restriction of sensitive access pending formal clarification of unexplained contact pattern.**

His fingers rested on the keyboard for a moment.

Then he saved it.

"Temporary," Daniel said.

Lena nodded slowly.

"Of course."

Jonas began opening her active tasks.

"The delivery-role review is with you," he said.

"Goes to you," Lena said.

"The question from the oversight office?"

"Don't open it while my access is suspended. Have it reassigned formally."

"Takes time."

"Yes."

Daniel heard the decision create work immediately.

Lena did not simply close her laptop. She went through each open item one by one.

One question to Jonas.

One document to Berg.

One timeline she was no longer allowed to maintain herself.

No dramatic scene.

Only a professional exclusion becoming real through small hand movements.

Then the access notice appeared on her screen.

**Authorization for sensitive project area temporarily suspended.**

Lena read it.

Her face stayed calm.

"Official enough now?" Daniel asked.

She looked at him.

"For the exclusion? Yes."

"I meant—"

"I know what you meant."

Daniel was silent.

Lena stood.

At the door, she stopped briefly.

"You didn't apply the rule incorrectly."

Daniel looked at her.

The sentence helped less than he expected.

"But?"

"No but."

She opened the door.

"If you need the rule, at least actually apply it."

Then she left.

Jonas was still sitting in front of the screen.

"What do we do with her hit?"

Daniel looked at Lena's name.

"Review it formally."

"And the others?"

"Continue as decided."

Berg nodded and returned to his place.

The room went back to work.

Daniel remained standing for a moment.

An hour earlier there had been twenty-seven names.

Now there were twenty-six names and Lena.

That was factually wrong. Of course she was still one of the twenty-seven.

That was exactly why Daniel noticed the thought.

He did not delete her hit.

He did not change the rule.

He simply could no longer pretend it felt the same when applied to every person.

The rule did.
