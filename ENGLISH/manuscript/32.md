## 32

Mara answered on the second ring.

"Hi."

Daniel was sitting in the car in the parking lot. He had not started the engine.

For the moment, the situation was stable enough that no one needed him.

That had been the definition of free time for days.

"Got a minute?"

"Yes."

He heard dishes and a cabinet door in the background.

"We're done for today."

"Good."

"Not done. But the acute part."

"Understood."

Daniel rubbed a hand over his face.

On the way to the car, he had told himself not to begin with the case.

That was exactly what he had done.

"How was your day?" he asked.

Mara was silent for a second.

"Interesting order."

"I'm practicing."

"Mediocre."

He laughed briefly.

Then it went quiet again.

"When do you have to give Hamburg your final answer?"

Mara did not answer immediately. The pause was enough.

"Today."

Daniel looked through the windshield at the dark windows of the building.

"Today by when?"

"Three hours ago."

"And?"

"I accepted."

Daniel rested his head against the seat.

There was no shock.

More like a brief emptiness in which his mind immediately started calculating.

"Start date still November first?"

"Yes."

"Apartment?"

"Two viewings next week."

"Furnished at first?"

"One of them."

"Commuting initially would still—"

Mara said nothing.

Daniel heard himself.

He stopped.

"Okay."

"You're allowed to ask."

"I know."

"I just wanted to let you finish calculating first."

"Was I that bad?"

"Start date, apartment, commuting in twelve seconds."

"Solid."

"For a freight company, maybe."

He laughed again, shorter this time.

Then he asked, "Are you happy?"

Mara exhaled.

"Yes."

The word did more than Daniel expected.

"Very?"

"Enough that I accepted."

"What does enough mean?"

"That I want the role. The team. The responsibility. And that I don't need a perfect solution to know I want to try it."

"What happens your first week?" Daniel asked.

Mara laughed softly.

"Is that a trick question?"

"No. I'm trying to understand what you're looking forward to."

This time the answer came faster.

"Two days of handover. Then a meeting with the new team. After that I go with my future boss to see a client. And somewhere in between I try to find out which apartment isn't directly on a six-lane road."

Daniel pictured her opening boxes in a strange kitchen while he sat in Berlin.

The image was surprisingly concrete.

"The furnished apartment?"

"Is practical."

"But?"

"It looks like a hotel that forgot people sometimes stay longer than three nights."

"Then the other one."

"More expensive."

Daniel was already opening his mouth.

Mara said, "No calculation."

He closed it again.

"I only wanted—"

"I know."

Daniel smiled despite everything.

"Difficult."

"You're learning."

"Slowly."

Mara told him about the second apartment. Small balcony. Longer commute to the office. But a room where she could actually fit a desk. She had not decided anything yet.

Daniel listened. Not all of it was about him.

He did not ask about square meters or lease duration.

"What's the first thing you're really looking forward to in Hamburg?"

She was quiet briefly.

"Is that a trick question?"

"No."

"Then probably the team. Building the structure myself and deciding how we work instead of walking into something finished and only fixing it."

Daniel heard the word *deciding* and this time let it stay where it was.

"And outside work?"

"Being able to walk out in the morning and get coffee, assuming the second apartment isn't completely lying."

"That's your big Hamburg picture?"

"Right now, yes."

He smiled.

It was ordinary enough that he understood how far Mara's decision had already grown beyond the abstract question of *Hamburg or Berlin*.

She was thinking about a team, a first client, a kitchen, a walk for coffee.

Until today, Daniel had mainly been thinking about distance.

"Sounds good," he said.

"It does."

This time he did not immediately turn it into a weekend schedule.

"And your team here?" he asked.

"I tell my boss tomorrow. Friday the others. My deputy gets more responsibility if everything works out."

"She happy about that?"

"First she'll tell me I'm crazy. Then she'll be happy."

Daniel knew the colleague only from two stories. He still noticed that Mara had already been building transitions while he was still thinking about when the two of them would find time to make a decision.

"I could come to Hamburg with you next weekend," he said.

Mara was quiet.

Daniel heard himself and added, "Not as a solution. Only if you want me to."

"That would be nice."

She made nothing larger out of it.

"I don't know how often I'll come back to Berlin at first," Mara said.

A calendar opened immediately in his head.

"Not at all the first two weeks?"

"No idea."

"After that every other—"

He stopped himself.

Mara said nothing.

"Never mind," Daniel said.

"What?"

"I'm trying to turn a relationship into a timetable."

"You're trying to find out whether it works in practice."

"That's not entirely wrong."

"No. Just a little early."

Daniel looked through the windshield.

"Then no weekend rule."

"Thank you."

"That sounded very relieved."

"It was."

He smiled.

Mara said, "I don't want to move to Hamburg and immediately have to prove every week that Hamburg isn't changing anything between us."

Daniel answered after a moment. "Something is changing."

"Yes."

"Doesn't mean it's over."

"No."

They did not have more certainty than that.

This time Daniel did not try to replace it with an appointment.

This time the calendar stayed closed. The pause was allowed to exist.

Mara heard it too.

"You're not calculating?"

"Trying not to."

"How does that feel?"

"Unprofessional."

She laughed softly.

Daniel asked, "What do you need from me in the first few weeks? Not organizationally."

Mara needed a moment.

"To be allowed to arrive without proving every week that Hamburg works for us."

Daniel looked at the dark windshield.

"So no review after two weeks."

"Please not."

"And if it's difficult?"

"Then it's difficult. That's not a decision against us yet."

Daniel nodded.

Normally he would have forced the uncertainty into a rhythm immediately: Friday evening train, Sunday back, every other weekend. A plan that looked reassuring simply because it existed.

Mara wanted something else.

Time in which the new reality was allowed to become real first.

"Okay," Daniel said.

This time she did not ask whether he meant it.

"And you?" Mara asked.

Daniel needed a moment.

"Me what?"

"What does your next week look like?"

He looked at the dark building in front of him.

"I don't know."

"At all?"

"Seven-thirty tomorrow morning. After that it depends on the situation."

Mara was quiet briefly.

The explanation was already prepared. He did not give it.

"Then you don't know," she said.

"Yes."

"That's information too."

Daniel smiled weakly.

"We're remarkably good today at honest bad answers."

"Maybe we need those more right now than good invented ones."

Daniel said nothing to that.

It was not a promise for Hamburg.

But it was a conversation in which Mara did not have to be the only one explaining her new life.

Nor did Daniel have to invent his.

Daniel looked at his hands.

She had told him that in the first conversation already, only differently. Back then he had asked about work from home.

"When are you telling your current team?"

"My boss tomorrow. Then the team."

"Resignation?"

"Prepared."

"Both apartment viewings next week?"

Mara was silent.

Daniel grimaced.

"Again."

"A little."

"Sorry."

"You don't have to apologize for thinking practically."

"Only if that's all I do."

"That would be a useful rule."

Daniel rested his head back.

"You could have texted me."

"I considered it."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because there was nothing you could have done except hang between two things."

"Maybe I could have said something."

"What?"

He thought about it.

Too long.

Mara said, "Exactly."

"That's unfair."

"Yes."

The answer came so quickly he almost laughed again.

"I didn't accept because you weren't there," she said. "I accepted because I want the job and because I had to decide today."

"Would you have decided differently if we'd had the appointment?"

Mara was silent for a moment.

"Maybe we would have understood better what it means for us."

"That wasn't the question."

"That's all I can honestly tell you."

Daniel nodded even though she could not see it.

No accusation.

No easy alternative past.

"And us?" he asked.

"We're not automatically gone."

"But?"

"But the question is different now."

"Not whether you're going anymore."

"Exactly."

"What matters is how we deal with it."

"Yes."

Daniel thought about train connections, weekends, two apartments, calendars.

He noticed the list and this time left it in his head.

"I don't know how right now," he said.

Mara was quiet briefly.

"At least that's an answer."

"Not a good one."

"It doesn't have to be today."

"You needed one today."

"For my job. Not for everything."

Daniel looked at the time and immediately turned his wrist away again.

"I'm happy for you," he said.

"I believe you."

"Doesn't sound like it."

"It does."

"Then what sounds like it doesn't?"

"That I'm tired anyway."

"Because of me?"

"Among other things. Hamburg too. And because I've spent days preparing decisions."

Daniel accepted that.

"I don't want you thinking I just let you go."

"I don't."

"Then what?"

"That you had good reasons to want to decide later."

"And?"

"My deadline still had a date."

Daniel was silent.

Mara did not have to explain the sentence.

"We'll talk tomorrow," he said.

Mara laughed softly once.

The laugh sounded tired, without malice.

Daniel grimaced.

"Wrong sentence."

"A little."

"I'll contact you tomorrow. No promise of a time."

"Better."

"And if it doesn't work again, I'll tell you beforehand."

"Even better."

This time they said goodbye.

Daniel put the phone on the passenger seat.

For a moment he felt something almost like relief.

A decision had been made.

It belonged to someone else.

Then he disliked himself for the relief.

He started the engine.

Mara was going to Hamburg.

What happened to the two of them remained open.

But the old version of their joint decision was not coming back.
