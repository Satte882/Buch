## 17

Berg did not ask whether Daniel had known the access was problematic.

He asked when Daniel had known.

"Before I ran the search."

Daniel sat opposite him. Lena was to his right, a closed laptop in front of her.

Berg had shut the door.

Three printouts lay on the table.

Daniel's note.

The current operational assessment.

The short technical assessment of the interrupted preparatory step.

Daniel had expected a fourth sheet.

A preliminary decision about him.

It never appeared.

"Not while you were doing it?" Berg asked.

"No."

"Not only after Lena asked?"

"No."

"Before you pressed Enter, you knew the use wasn't properly covered."

"Yes."

Berg leaned back.

"Good."

Lena looked at him.

"What's good about that?"

"That we don't also have to talk about self-deception."

"It doesn't make the access better."

"I didn't say it did."

Daniel slid his note closer to Berg.

He had written it while the operation was still unfolding. Time. Plate. Data set. Purpose of the check. Result. No wording that turned *not properly covered* into *unclear*.

Berg read the whole thing.

He did not limit himself to the highlighted parts.

Daniel heard footsteps in the corridor outside.

Someone stopped briefly in front of the door, then moved on.

"You limited it to the plate. You didn't expand to people or pull any additional data?"

"Only the hit associations and the two timestamps."

"No screenshot, no export?"

"No."

"Why keep it that narrow?"

Daniel looked at him.

"Because I knew the plate check itself was already too far."

Berg put the paper down.

Lena said, "The limitation matters. It isn't authorization."

"I understand that too."

"Then say it once without using the word success."

Berg looked at her.

"What?"

"Evaluate his access as if we'd found nothing afterward."

The room went quiet.

Daniel looked at Berg's hand. Two fingers rested on his note.

Berg thought.

"Then Daniel would have used data from a different purpose context for this case without a proper basis."

"Thank you."

"And I would have initiated an internal review."

"Now too?"

"Yes."

Lena nodded.

Daniel realized he was waiting for a harder continuation.

*And until then, you're off the case.*

It did not come.

Berg looked at Daniel.

"If you hadn't run the check?"

Lena answered first.

"Then we don't know what would have happened."

"I'm asking Daniel."

Daniel looked at the table.

"The current link to the garage probably would have come later."

"How much later?"

"I can't answer that seriously."

"Would we have stopped the minibus?"

"Maybe."

"In time?"

"Maybe not."

Berg pulled the operational assessment toward him.

"The assessment says the preparatory step was interrupted in time."

"Yes."

"And the regular inquiries?"

"Two were still outstanding when I searched."

"When did the first come back?"

Daniel did not know from memory.

Lena did.

"8:18 p.m. No solid vehicle connection."

Berg looked at the time of the first independent garage hit.

8:07 p.m.

Eleven minutes earlier.

"And the second?"

"8:34 p.m. Partial connection through the same company network, but still no garage."

Berg leaned back.

"So the rule violation bought real time."

Lena folded her hands.

"And that's exactly why the outcome can't become retroactive authorization."

"I'm not doing that."

"Then what are you doing?"

Berg looked at Daniel.

"I'm not removing him from the case."

Daniel looked at Berg.

"If the hit had produced nothing, would you leave me on the case?"

Lena looked up from the laptop.

Berg did not answer immediately.

"Maybe," he said at last.

"That's thin."

"It's the honest answer."

Berg pushed the operational assessment aside.

"The operational question would have been the same: what do I lose by removing you from the case immediately, and what additional risk do I create by keeping you?"

"And without the success?"

"The second risk would have been harder to justify."

Lena said, "So the success does matter."

"Of course it matters to the operational decision," Berg said. "It just can't replace the rule assessment."

Daniel leaned back.

It was exactly the kind of distinction that sounded reasonable and still had an effect.

The successful rule violation made him more operationally valuable in the same situation in which that rule violation was supposed to be reviewed.

No acquittal.

But no neutral starting point either.

Daniel looked again at his service ID.

"Then the absence of a sanction is itself a consequence of the success."

Berg tightened his mouth briefly.

"The absence of an immediate sanction. Don't make it more than that."

Daniel nodded.

That was enough to make the distinction uncomfortable.

He had expected the sentence.

It still felt wrong.

He had prepared himself for the consequence without consciously deciding to. Since the start of the meeting, both his feet had been planted firmly on the floor as if he might have to stand up at any moment. His service ID lay on the table. Usually it stayed in his jacket pocket. He had taken it out before Berg arrived.

Now he looked at it.

So did Lena.

Berg followed their eyes.

"What?"

Daniel slid the ID one centimeter back toward himself.

"Nothing."

"You expected me to take it?"

"At least to remove me from the case."

Berg said nothing.

Daniel did not take the ID off the table.

"Why not?" he asked.

Berg exhaled.

"Because right now I have to separate two decisions you'd rather turn into one."

"Which ones?"

"Whether your access was acceptable. And whether taking you out of the current operation now is smart."

Lena said, "That's an operational decision."

"Yes."

"Not an assessment of the access."

"Yes."

"Then I want it documented exactly that way."

"Do it."

Daniel looked at her.

Lena opened her laptop.

"I will."

Berg continued.

"We have an ongoing cross-state operation. Daniel knows the sequence, the source, and the open connections. The intervention exposed a real danger. If I remove him on principle now, I lose operational continuity in a situation where time is exactly our problem."

"You also lose a signal," Lena said.

Berg looked at her.

"Which one?"

"That a boundary remains a boundary when breaking it works."

"I know."

"Then don't pretend continuity is free."

Berg was silent.

Daniel stayed out of it.

It was not his argument to win.

Berg eventually looked at him.

"I can take you off the case now. Then in an hour we hand it to someone who knows the source line, the warehouse, the service provider, and the two vehicle strands from the files. That's possible."

Daniel nodded.

"And maybe right," Berg said.

"Yes."

"Or I keep you on the operation, document the violation, run the review separately, and put you under an explicit reporting requirement for any further exceptional decisions, as far as time allows."

Lena said, "As far as time allows is a very large door."

"Do you want me to require absolute prior authorization in an acute situation?"

"No."

"Then the wording stays uncomfortable."

Lena did not answer.

Berg pulled Daniel's note back toward himself and wrote a short file note in the upper right.

"Continues on operation for reasons of operational continuity," Lena read.

Berg put down the pen.

"And underneath: separate review of data use."

"Good," Lena said.

Daniel stared at the two sentences.

That was how dry a moment could look when, for the past hour, it had meant the question of whether he would still be part of the case tomorrow.

"Who reviews it?" he asked.

Berg looked at Lena, then back at Daniel.

"Not you. Not me alone. That's enough for now."

"When?"

"As soon as the situation allows."

A laugh rose in Daniel and died somewhere before reaching his mouth. The same phrase as the reporting requirement. Everything depended on time now.

"And until then?"

"Until then you keep working. But today's success doesn't give you a silent blank check."

"I don't want one."

Lena looked at him.

"The problem isn't whether you want it. The problem is whether you start assuming you have it."

Daniel did not answer.

The sentence sat where a sanction might have sat.

Maybe that was the more uncomfortable consequence.

Berg leaned forward.

"Daniel. This is not an invitation."

"I didn't take it as one."

"Good. If you see another boundary, you tell me beforehand whenever time allows it at all."

Daniel thought of 7:24 p.m.

Three minutes to the hit.

He could have called Berg first.

He had.

Berg had not answered.

That did not make the decision cleaner.

It only made Berg's new rule less clear than it sounded.

"If time allows," Daniel said.

Berg heard the qualification.

He left it there.

"Operational briefing tomorrow, seven-thirty."

That should have ended the meeting.

Daniel stayed seated.

Lena kept typing.

Berg stood, noticed Daniel was not moving, and stopped.

"What?"

"That's it?" Daniel asked.

Berg looked at him.

"What were you expecting?"

"A consequence."

"There may still be one."

"May?"

"There will be a review. I don't know what consequence it will have."

"But not now."

"Not now."

Daniel looked at his service ID again.

He had put it there ready to hand over.

Now that felt almost embarrassing.

He picked it up and slipped it into his jacket pocket.

The movement felt larger than it was.

Berg took Daniel's note.

"If it makes you feel any better: I don't like this decision."

Daniel stood.

"Neither do I."

Berg opened the door.

"Unfortunately, it worked."

No one said anything.

Lena closed her laptop.

Daniel did not see the file on her screen. He only noticed that she skipped the normal shared case area and moved her notes into a different workspace before locking the computer.

"What was that?" he asked.

Lena looked at him.

"Documentation?"

"Separate. Berg just said the review is being handled separately."

Daniel held her gaze.

"Who has access?"

"Not the whole operational team."

She said nothing more.

It was professionally plausible.

And still a new information path Daniel could not fully see.

Lena passed him on the way to the door.

"You wanted a consequence," she said.

"Yes."

"Documentation is one."

Then she left.

Lena had barely left the room when Daniel's work phone vibrated.

Jonas.

"Can you still release the current vehicle status?"

Daniel looked at the screen.

His access still worked.

No lock. No warning. No role switched to **temporarily revoked**.

Only the normal case, the same button as an hour earlier.

"Yes," Daniel said.

"You're still on the case?"

Daniel looked at the door Lena had gone through.

"Apparently."

He did not like the word.

He opened the vehicle status, checked source and timestamp, and released only the current, independently confirmed portion.

Routine.

That was what made it strange.

After the conversation with Berg, Daniel had expected some visible interruption. A different room. A formal handover. At least one moment in which his rule violation stopped the work.

Instead, Jonas asked him about a vehicle.

Daniel completed the task.

Then he looked at the access list. His name was still everywhere it had been before.

The consequence existed as a note.

Operationally, that evening, it was almost invisible.

Daniel wrote down the time at which Berg had decided to keep him on the operation.

Distrust of Berg was not the reason. Daniel did not want to tell himself later that the lack of an immediate sanction had somehow simply happened.

That too had been a decision.

Then Daniel opened his own note once more.

At the top was the rule violation.

Beneath it, Berg's decision to keep him on the operation.

Two separate paragraphs. Two separate responsibilities.

Daniel added no sentence between them that turned the second into retroactive approval of the first.

He left the gap visible.

That was where the discomfort lived.

Daniel stayed alone in the room for a moment.

Nothing remained on the table from his access except the faint imprint of paper in the dust and an empty water glass.

He had crossed a boundary.

He had admitted it openly.

He had probably protected people because of it.

And tomorrow at seven-thirty he would walk back into the same operation.

None of those sentences made any of the others untrue.
