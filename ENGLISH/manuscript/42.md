## 42

Jana opened the door herself.

Daniel had not expected that.

"You're here?"

"Obviously."

She stood in the doorway of her practice. Jeans, sweater, hair tied back. The hall behind her was lit, but the treatment rooms were dark.

"I thought you were still with Claudia."

"I was."

"And now?"

"I'm here."

Daniel automatically looked down the street.

Jana noticed.

"There isn't a man with binoculars standing in the doorway across the street."

"Very reassuring."

"Wasn't meant to be."

She let him in.

Two cups stood on the table in the small break room. One clean, one not.

"Claudia?" Daniel asked.

"Just left."

"Alone?"

Jana looked at him.

Daniel took off his jacket.

"Okay."

She sat down.

"Is it over?"

Daniel took the chair opposite her.

"The acute threat is."

"What does acute mean?"

"That the concrete strand I told you to change your routines because of has been stopped."

"And the rest?"

"Still under investigation."

Jana nodded.

"Was I a target?"

This time Daniel answered immediately.

"We can't establish that."

Jana waited.

"And the observation?"

"That was real. It probably didn't come from the perpetrator network."

"Say that again and listen to what it sounds like."

Daniel looked at the dirty cup.

"I know."

He explained as much as he could.

Someone from inside their own institutional environment had probably arranged for Daniel to be observed before the case. Later, knowledge about Jana had been used to increase the pressure on him. The external threat had still been real.

Jana watched him for a long time.

"Probably used," she said finally. "What does that mean?"

Daniel put his hands on the table.

"That the information about you was real. Someone knew when you left the building and which routes you took."

"I knew that. What's new?"

"That we have no solid indication the people behind the external threat selected you as a target in your own right."

Jana waited.

Daniel had to build the next sentence more carefully.

"The private reference mainly did something to me. It narrowed my decision space."

"So I was a means to an end."

"Probably in that part."

"Probably."

"I can't say more cleanly than that."

She stood, went to the counter, then came back without taking anything.

"That's almost worse than a clear answer."

"I know."

"No. You like unclear answers professionally. I have to live with them."

Daniel said nothing.

She was right enough that any defense would have sounded wrong.

Jana sat again.

"What part of your warning back then would you give me again today?"

Daniel thought.

"That there was a real reason for caution."

"And what wouldn't you say again?"

"That maximum caution is automatically the right decision."

Jana nodded slowly.

"I can do more with that."

She listened without interrupting him.

"So you didn't know whether I was in danger."

"No."

"And to this day you don't know whether I needed to leave my apartment."

Daniel looked at the dirty cup.

"No."

Jana leaned back.

No triumph.

Only fatigue.

"I still wouldn't have simply carried on like before," she said.

"I know."

"I didn't know that then either."

Daniel looked at her.

"I thought you were about to say I overreacted."

"You probably did."

"Thanks."

"And I probably made it smaller because I didn't want you taking over everything again."

Daniel nodded slowly.

They sat in silence for a moment.

Then he said, "For the next few days I'd still—"

He stopped.

Jana raised her eyebrows.

"What?"

Daniel took out his phone and opened the short information he was allowed to give her.

"There is no current concrete threat information about you. A few connections are still open. Changing routine routes reduces a residual risk. That's all I can honestly tell you."

Jana did not take the phone.

"I can work with that."

Daniel put it away again.

"What do you need from me?" he asked.

Jana thought longer than he expected.

"A rule that applies to information. Not to me."

Daniel waited.

"If you get something new that increases my concrete risk, you tell me exactly what changed."

"As far as I'm allowed to."

"Yes."

"And if all we have is another theory?"

"Then you don't automatically tell me to make my life even narrower."

Daniel nodded.

"If the observation happens again?"

"Then that's new information."

"If someone internal just says it might?"

"Then it's a somebody-says-it-might."

Daniel had to smile briefly.

"Technical term?"

"You should introduce it."

Jana became serious again.

"I don't want less information. I just don't want every uncertainty to arrive as an instruction to me."

Daniel looked at her phone, the calendar, the practice around her.

"Understood."

This time, giving information did not mean giving away responsibility.

It meant giving Jana enough of it for the decision to genuinely remain hers.

Jana looked at him as though she had not expected the question.

"Right now?"

"Yes."

"No additional rules."

Daniel nodded.

"Information if something changes."

"Yes."

"And if you don't know something, don't automatically give me the more dangerous version just because it sounds safer."

Daniel had to look away briefly.

"That'll be harder."

"Then practice."

"Very constructive."

"You're welcome."

Jana took a sheet of paper from the table and wrote down three points.

"So you don't ask again immediately."

Daniel looked.

**Claudia at closing. Vary route. Changes only with new information.**

"What does the last one mean?"

"That I don't invent a new protective measure every day just because theoretically we could do more."

Daniel nodded.

"And if I tell you the situation has worsened?"

"Then tell me why."

"And if all I can say is that it's less clear?"

Jana looked at him.

"Then I decide with unclear information."

Daniel had to smile briefly.

"Sounds familiar."

"It should."

She slid the paper toward him, then immediately pulled it back.

"This is not clearance for your file, by the way."

"Shame."

"My rules."

"Understood."

"For real this time?"

Daniel nodded.

The difference looked small and yet reached into every decision she would make next: no checklist to work through anymore, only the current state. What Jana did with it remained hers.

She stood and opened the small calendar on the wall.

Monday was full.

Two appointments in the morning, three in the afternoon, an administrative block in between.

Daniel had spent so many days thinking in time windows that the ordinary calendar almost irritated him.

"You're really planning to just work."

"Yes."

"Good."

Jana looked at him.

"That was almost permission."

"It wasn't."

"Close."

Daniel let it stand.

"Good."

"I'm still staying closed tomorrow."

Jana reached for her own phone.

"I told Claudia I won't close alone for the next three evenings."

Daniel started to say something.

She raised one finger.

"My decision."

"I understood."

"And I won't take the same route every time."

"You already said that."

"I'm repeating it so you don't add three more things."

Daniel leaned back.

"Fair."

"Monday I open normally. If something changes before then, you tell me exactly what. Not just that I should be careful."

"Yes."

"And if nothing changes, I keep deciding for myself."

Daniel nodded.

This time she said nothing about it.

Daniel almost nodded again, then stopped himself.

"And Monday?"

"I work."

"Alone?"

"Daniel."

"Information, not instruction."

She had to laugh briefly.

"Claudia's there. And for the next few days I won't drive exactly the same route every evening."

"Okay."

Jana stood and picked up the dirty cup.

"Oh. The apartment."

Daniel looked at her.

"What about it?"

"The realtor put in the new price. Photographer was there yesterday. No notary appointment yet."

"Without me?"

She stopped by the sink.

"You sent me the power of attorney."

Daniel remembered.

At some point between two situation briefings.

"Right."

"I used it."

"Apparently."

"The photographer was a disaster, by the way," Jana said.

Daniel blinked.

"What?"

"Apartment. Photos. He wanted the kitchen to look bigger and took the door off its hinges."

Daniel laughed, though he had not expected to.

"Did he put it back?"

"After discussion."

"Then you were adequately protected."

"Very funny."

Jana rinsed the cup.

Their mother's apartment had continued to exist while Daniel had believed the case was consuming every available reality: realtor, photos, power of attorney, another appointment. None of it was dramatic. It was simply life that did not need clearance from the situation he was managing.

Jana put the cup in the sink.

"You can come to the next appointment if you want."

"Send me the time."

She turned toward him.

Daniel raised a hand.

"I didn't say you should wait for me."

"Progress."

"Don't exaggerate."

This time she really laughed.

Daniel stayed another ten minutes.

Jana told him about two patients she absolutely wanted to see Monday.

She did not discuss their reasons, only that she had moved appointments and one of them had already called twice.

Daniel listened.

It was nothing he could solve.

That was exactly why it felt good.

When his phone vibrated once, he checked the display.

No acute report.

He put it away again.

Jana noticed but did not comment.

Then he left.

Jana closed the door behind him.

Not because he had told her to.
