# 3

"You turned three doors into a group."

Lena Vogt set her coffee on the table without looking. A drop ran down the side of the cup and landed on the printout.

Daniel pulled the page away.

"Thanks."

"You're welcome."

Jonas sat across from them, failing to hide a grin.

Three access points to an administrative building were marked on the monitor. Two side doors, one delivery entrance. The night before, the access system had logged three different contractor badges there.

Within eleven minutes.

All three companies worked in the building regularly. None had been scheduled for that time.

Daniel had placed the three entries side by side because each looked ordinary on its own. Together they looked more conspicuous.

Company one in through the west side door.

Four minutes later, company two at the delivery entrance.

Seven minutes after that, company three at the east side door.

Jonas had first asked whether the companies shared the same client. They didn't.

Daniel had already drafted a short note. Not sent yet. **Possible coordinated use of multiple external access points outside normal operating hours.**

The sentence sat in the draft looking more objective than the evidence behind it.

"Three badges," Daniel said. "Not three doors."

"Even worse."

Lena pulled over a chair.

"What's your working hypothesis?"

She was a lawyer, but in meetings like this she never acted as if her job began after the operational decision had been made. She preferred asking the uncomfortable questions early, before everyone had settled on a direction.

Daniel pointed to the times.

"Someone is testing access points. Or coordinating people who want to know which doors will open at night."

"Why?"

"Three companies. Same time window. Different sides of the building."

"That's the rationale. Not the why."

Daniel leaned back.

"Because that's what it looks like."

"Yes." Lena picked up her coffee. "It does."

Jonas looked from one to the other.

Lena pulled the printout closer.

"What else would have to be true for your story to hold?"

Daniel pointed to the companies. "That the people weren't there for a normal reason."

"That would help."

"And that the times are right."

"Also."

"And that it really was three different people."

Jonas said, "The badges are assigned to three people."

Lena looked at him. "That wasn't the question."

Jonas twisted his mouth. "I'm starting to understand why you two like each other."

"Do we?" Lena asked.

Daniel didn't react.

He opened the detailed access view. The three records looked identical. Reader ID, badge number, time, result: access granted.

Rows that looked alike had a habit of making different things look more alike than they were.

"What single finding would hurt your version most?" she asked.

Daniel looked back at the timeline.

Three timestamps. Eleven minutes. Three doors.

If the people hadn't actually been there at the same time, almost nothing would remain of his group.

Then he stopped at the timestamps.

"If these aren't the actual door-open times."

Lena said nothing.

Jonas leaned forward. "They come from the access-control system."

"From three readers," Daniel said. "Are they synchronized?"

Jonas opened the technical status. "They should be."

"Not the same thing."

"I'll call."

While Jonas looked for the technical on-call contact, Daniel went through the rest of the metadata. Two readers had maintenance flags in the system history from the evening before. Not a fault. Maintenance.

He showed Lena the entries.

"Could mean anything," she said.

"Yes."

"You sound disappointed."

"Not yet."

"Good answer."

Daniel reopened his draft.

**Possible coordinated use.**

He highlighted the word *coordinated*.

"Too early?" Jonas asked, still on hold.

Daniel didn't delete it.

"It's still a hypothesis."

Lena looked at the screen. "Then make sure it stays one."

Ten minutes later, a technician joined them by video and explained that two of the three readers had temporarily been offline after maintenance the previous evening. The events had been stored locally and transmitted when the readers reconnected.

"Which timestamp does our default view show?" Daniel asked.

The technician shared his screen. "Up here is server receipt. Down here is the local event."

The local timestamp was smaller and gray.

Jonas stared at it. "Why is the less important time bigger?"

"Because the interface was originally built for fault analysis."

"Of course."

"For you, the local event matters more."

"We just figured that out."

The display in front of Jonas emphasized the time the event reached the server more strongly than the original local timestamp.

Two of the three supposed nighttime entries had happened that afternoon.

Daniel had the two original events exported and compared the badge numbers. Different people. But hours apart.

His group was gone.

He looked at the unsent draft.

One sentence. Three apparently clean data points. And now the central verb was already wrong.

Daniel placed the cursor after **Possible**.

Then he deleted the entire line.

"Not going to salvage it?" Lena asked.

"What am I supposed to salvage?"

"You could write: multiple contractor entries with one unexplained nighttime access."

"Could."

"It would even be true."

Daniel looked at her.

"And it would sound as if the other two were still relevant to the nighttime access."

Lena nodded once.

"Good."

Daniel opened a new note and entered only the one record that remained.

11:18 p.m. Delivery entrance. Contractor badge. Assignment completed.

Nothing more.

Jonas stared at the screen.

"That's ugly."

"That's software," Lena said.

"Same thing."

Daniel was only half listening.

One entry remained.

11:18 p.m. Delivery entrance. Badge from a maintenance company whose work had ended at five.

The single entry almost looked more suspicious now because the other two no longer built a story around it.

Daniel noticed the thought and pushed it aside.

"We keep that one," he said.

Lena looked at him. "Why?"

"Because it stands alone now."

"That doesn't make it more suspicious."

"No. Just testable."

She nodded.

Daniel called the maintenance company. The first contact knew nothing. The second remembered that a shift supervisor had driven back to the building that evening. He'd forgotten some tools.

"Which shift supervisor?"

The name didn't match the badge.

Jonas looked up.

"There's your loose end."

Daniel wrote down both names.

"Or my next mistake."

They pulled the video from the delivery entrance. The man in the footage was the shift supervisor the company had named. He carried a tool bag inside and brought it back out twelve minutes later.

"Why the wrong badge?" Jonas asked.

Security had the answer. A shared tray held several access cards for outside contractors. The shift supervisor had taken the wrong one. Not allowed. Not unusual enough to turn into coordinated reconnaissance.

No reconnaissance. No group. No coordinated operation.

But a real violation of the access rules.

Daniel updated the case.

He didn't delete his first hypothesis from the notes. Underneath it he added the corrected version: **Two timestamps misinterpreted. One genuine unauthorized badge use with no link to a security threat.**

Jonas read over his shoulder.

"Three people turned into one guy who can't read his badge."

"He had the wrong badge."

"That's what I said."

"And we report the shared badge tray to building security."

"So there was something after all."

Daniel looked at him.

Jonas grinned. "I'm learning."

Lena was already at the door.

"You were right about the access," she said.

Daniel looked up.

"And wrong about the rest."

"That too."

"Doesn't that bother you?" Jonas asked.

Daniel looked at the three original lines. Eleven minutes. Three doors. A group that had never existed.

"It would be worse if it didn't."

Lena stayed at the door for another moment.

"That's the job," she said.

"I can work with that."

She left.
