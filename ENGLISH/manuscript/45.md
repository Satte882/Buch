## 45

Mara no longer had a coffee machine.

Daniel stood in her kitchen in Hamburg looking at a small porcelain pour-over dripper.

"You're serious?"

"For six weeks now."

"Voluntarily?"

"I've survived things here you know nothing about."

She switched on the kettle.

The apartment was smaller than the one they had shared in Berlin, but it did not feel temporary. That had surprised Daniel on his first visit.

Pictures on the walls. Books on the shelves. Shoes in the hallway. A plant stand by the window that Mara would never have bought in Berlin because there had been no room for it.

Nothing was waiting for her to go back.

That morning Mara had already shown him her route to work without meaning to explain it. She knew the bakery downstairs, greeted the man at the kiosk, and stopped at a traffic light even though Daniel had already taken two steps into the street.

"They'll actually run over your feet here," she said.

"In Berlin too."

"You just don't notice there."

Outside her office, a colleague was waiting with two paper cups.

"You must be Daniel."

Mara introduced them briefly.

The two of them immediately began discussing an appointment. Daniel understood only that someone could not make Wednesday and that this had been a problem since yesterday.

Mara did not unpack it for him.

She said goodbye to the colleague and continued walking with Daniel.

"Nice," he said.

"Yes."

"How long have you been working together?"

"Since my second day."

Daniel automatically counted backward, then stopped himself.

There was no meaningful point at which he could catch up on everything that had become important without him.

"I'm having dinner with the team at seven tonight," she said.

Daniel looked at her.

"Tonight?"

"It's been in the calendar since Tuesday."

He took out his phone.

It was.

"I thought that was next week."

"Of course."

"Can I come?"

"If you want."

"Do you want me to?"

Mara thought for a moment.

"Yes. But not if you get up at seven-thirty and leave because Berlin texts you."

"Fair."

"Wasn't a condition."

"Sounded like one."

"Then I learned from you."

Daniel made a face.

His phone vibrated.

A work message.

He read the first line.

A question about the integrated review. No operation. No acute case.

He would once have answered because it would take two minutes.

Mara put the coffee in front of him.

"Do you have to?"

"No."

"Then do what you want."

She sat down.

Daniel tapped the message anyway.

**Seen. Monday is fine.**

Then he put the phone face down.

Mara did not comment.

That afternoon, the plant stand was crooked.

Mara had noticed for days and ignored it.

Daniel knelt beside it and tightened a screw.

"You don't have to rescue that."

"It wobbles."

"For three weeks."

"That doesn't make it better."

"It didn't bother me."

Daniel stopped.

"Want me to stop?"

Mara looked up from the sofa.

"No. You're already doing it now."

He kept tightening the screw.

It was absurdly small.

He still noticed that he had asked.

He had started asking first whether something was his problem at all, rather than whether he was solving it correctly.

Mara said nothing about that either.

When the stand was straight, Daniel put the screwdriver back in the drawer where Mara now kept it. By his third visit, he already knew where that was.

Not home yet. But long past being a guest in a stranger's apartment.

"How's the apartment search for your team?" he asked.

"What apartment search?"

"You said two people are still moving here."

"Oh. One found something in Altona. The other is commuting for now."

Daniel nodded.

There were now many people in Mara's daily life whom he knew only from half-sentences. She was not hiding them from him. He simply had not been there when they became important.

"Next week I'll come Saturday," he said.

"I may be working Saturday morning."

"Then Friday."

Mara looked at him.

"On Friday I don't know yet whether Friday works."

Daniel laughed.

"That's my answer."

"It was."

"Unpleasant."

"A little."

They drank coffee.

Later, they walked to the subway because Mara wanted to show him a store where she had seen a table.

Just before the team dinner, Mara's phone rang.

She looked at the display.

"I have to take this."

Daniel nodded.

Mara stepped aside and spent five minutes talking about a presentation, a rescheduled appointment, and someone who had been waiting for a decision since that morning.

Daniel understood enough to realize she was no longer simply new in Hamburg.

She was part of something there now.

When she hung up, he said, "Problem solved?"

"For today."

"Do you still have to send something?"

"Maybe later."

Daniel waited for the old movement inside himself: change the plan, reorder the time, turn a maybe into a fixed task.

It came.

He let it pass.

"Then let's go eat."

Mara looked at him briefly.

"Yes."

Daniel did not have to make more out of it.

Daniel thought the table was too big.

Mara did too.

They did not buy anything.

Later, on the way to dinner, Daniel asked, "What do we do about Christmas?"

Mara looked at him from the side.

"It's September."

"That's why I'm asking early."

"You're asking early so you can check a decision off the list."

"Possible."

They kept walking.

"I don't know," Mara said. "My sister will probably go to Cologne. I don't know what my work schedule will be yet."

Daniel nodded.

"Then we don't know."

"That was easy."

"Don't get used to it."

Mara smiled.

It was no solution for the next few years.

Only a weekend, a team dinner, a crooked plant stand, and a question that was allowed to stay open.

That evening Daniel went with her to the team dinner.

His phone vibrated twice in his jacket pocket.

The first time he did not look.

The second time he did.

Nothing urgent.

He put it away again.

The next morning, he drove back to Berlin.

Mara stayed in Hamburg.

That was how it was now.
