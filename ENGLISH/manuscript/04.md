## 4

The clear plastic strip on his mother's mailbox was sticking out by a millimeter.

Maybe two.

Daniel stopped in the hallway.

Jana was already half a flight of stairs ahead.

"What?"

He did not answer right away.

The mailboxes were old, painted beige, their edges darkened by years of use. The label still read **REUTER**, even though their mother had been dead for almost nine months.

Jana had replaced the paper strip two weeks earlier after the old one slipped behind the plastic.

Now the right corner no longer sat flush.

Daniel ran a fingernail underneath it.

"Did you open this?"

Jana came back down two steps. "What?"

"The label."

She looked at it.

"No."

"You sure?"

"Daniel."

"I'm just asking."

"You never just ask."

She went upstairs again.

Daniel did not pull the plastic out. He only checked the edge. No fresh damage. No scratch. Nothing that meant more than a badly seated piece of plastic in a forty-year-old mailbox.

Beside the mailboxes was the building's intercom. Also old. Three buttons had newer name labels stuck over them; one was missing its cover entirely. Daniel did not start pressing anything. He only looked.

The front door closed behind him. Not quite. The closer held it open for a moment, then pulled it shut.

A man from the ground floor came down the stairs, nodded to Daniel, and went out.

"Evening."

"Evening."

The man did not look at the mailbox.

Daniel waited until the door had closed again.

Then he went upstairs.

On the second floor, Jana was waiting with the key in her hand.

"You coming?"

He came.

To Daniel, the apartment still smelled like his mother, even though almost everything that could have explained the smell had long since been packed into boxes. Jana insisted it was the wood in the old cabinets.

The small shelf where the mail used to sit was gone from the hallway. Its outline was still visible on the lighter patch of wallpaper.

"Where's the shelf?" Daniel asked.

Jana closed the door behind them. "Charity store."

"When?"

"Saturday."

"By yourself?"

She looked at him.

"With four armed men."

"Jana."

"With the driver from the charity store. Is that enough?"

Daniel took off his jacket.

A binder lay on the dining table beside two printouts and a pen.

The small ceramic bowl their mother used to keep keys in was still on the windowsill. Jana picked it up, held it for a moment, then put it into an open box on the floor.

Daniel watched her.

"That too?"

"Yes."

"You liked that."

"Yes."

"Then why get rid of it?"

Jana looked into the box. "Because I don't have to keep everything I like."

Daniel said nothing.

She closed the box as if that settled the question.

Jana did not sit down right away. She went to the window, raised the half-closed blind, and put two glasses of water on the table.

"The realtor wants to know by Friday whether we'll lower the price," Jana said.

Daniel took off his jacket.

"By how much?"

"Twenty thousand."

"Why?"

"Because we've been trying to sell for four months, and because the kitchen looks like somebody stopped making decisions in 1997."

Daniel looked toward the kitchen.

"Mom liked it."

"Mom didn't have to sell it."

Jana sat down.

Daniel remained standing.

"Was anyone here today?"

She put down the pen.

"Not again."

"Because of the mailbox."

"The super. The cleaners. A neighbor. Someone who put a letter in it."

"You don't have to open the label to put a letter in."

"Maybe nobody opened it."

"It was flush."

Jana looked at him for a moment.

"And you know that because?"

Daniel did know. The last time they had been there, he had pressed the strip back into place himself while Jana was on the phone with the realtor.

He did not say so.

"Because I notice things."

"Exactly."

She picked up the pen again.

"Price. Yes or no?"

Daniel finally sat down.

He read the numbers. Comparable sales, offer, fees. The buyers wanted to get to the notary quickly. Jana wanted it finished.

The realtor had attached three comparable sales. Two were below their current asking price, one was higher. Daniel opened the details on his phone.

"This one has eight square meters less."

"And a balcony that doesn't face the trash cans."

"Ground floor, though."

"Daniel."

"I'm looking at it."

"You've been looking at it for four months."

He set the phone on the table.

"I'd wait another week."

"Of course."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"That you'd still wait another week."

"Seven days isn't a catastrophe for an apartment at this price."

"It's not about seven days."

Daniel looked back at the papers.

Jana brushed a strand of hair from her face. She looked tired. Not angry. That was worse.

"Since January," she said. "Clearing the place out, realtor, estate paperwork, insurance. Every time you say you want to make a clean decision. Then I have three appointments and you have one new reason why we shouldn't decide yet."

"I'm here today."

"Yes. And you're checking the mailbox."

Daniel put down the papers.

"Something was different."

"Could be."

"You didn't see it."

"No." Jana shrugged. "And if somebody touched the name label, I still want to sell the apartment."

Daniel looked toward the apartment door.

"Who has a key?"

Jana closed her eyes for a moment.

"Me. You. The realtor for scheduled appointments. Nobody else."

"The super?"

"No."

"Cleaners?"

"Only if somebody lets them in."

"When was the realtor here last?"

"Day before yesterday."

"With who?"

"Prospective buyers."

"How many?"

Jana put down the pen.

"Do you want his visitor list now?"

Daniel did not answer right away.

That was enough.

"You've got nothing," she said.

"I didn't say I did."

"You've spent three minutes turning a crooked piece of paper into access routes."

"I'm checking whether there's a simple explanation."

"The simple explanation is old plastic."

"Maybe."

"And that's exactly why we're not going through everyone who's been in this building since January."

Daniel noticed how much the word *maybe* bothered him.

Not because Jana was wrong. Because the explanation could not be verified without creating more work than the thing justified.

He went to the door, opened it again, and looked into the hall.

Empty.

Downstairs, the front door buzzed. Someone came in. Grocery bags rustled. Then footsteps on the stairs.

From two floors up, Daniel could barely see the mailbox anymore.

He closed the apartment door.

"Okay," he said.

Jana looked up.

"What's okay?"

"Mailbox."

"You're letting it live?"

"For now."

"Brave."

He almost smiled. Did not.

Jana pulled a second sheet from the binder.

"One more thing."

Daniel looked at it.

A list of things from the apartment. Keep, sell, donate, throw away. Several items already had check marks beside them.

"You've already decided this?"

"Some of it."

"The radio?"

"Donate."

"It still works."

"That's why donate."

Daniel ran a finger down the list. The old kitchen table. Two lamps. The armchair in the bedroom.

"I still wanted to look at the chair."

"Since February."

"Maybe somebody in the family wants it."

"Who?"

Daniel said nothing.

Jana took the sheet back from him.

"See?"

"What?"

"You don't want to keep everything. You just don't want to give anything away for good."

Daniel leaned back.

"Now you're analyzing me."

"No. I'm arranging bulk trash pickup for Saturday."

She pushed the binder toward him.

"I'm telling the realtor tomorrow that I'll set the new price with him on Friday. If you want to be there, I'll send you the time. If not, I'll do it."

"You need my approval."

"You can give it digitally."

"Jana."

"What?"

"I don't want you deciding this alone."

"Then decide with me."

"I'm sitting right here."

"No. You're sitting next to the decision asking for another week."

Daniel picked up the comparison again.

"Twenty thousand isn't nothing."

"No."

"And if a better offer comes in next week?"

"Then I'll be glad we haven't been to the notary yet. If it doesn't, we'll be one week further along having the same conversation."

"You don't know that."

"Right."

Jana leaned back.

"All I know is that for nine months I keep coming back here. At some point I don't want to know which scratch in Mom's hallway is new."

Daniel looked at the lighter patch where the shelf had been.

It was the first sentence that evening he had no way to check.

He had no good answer.

She took the binder back.

"Send me the papers Friday before you decide," he said.

Jana nodded. "I will."

"And if I can't respond—"

"Then I decide."

Daniel wanted to object.

He did not.

He took the list again before Jana slid it back into the binder.

"One thing."

"Which?"

He pointed to the ceramic bowl from the hallway.

"I'd keep that."

Jana looked toward the box.

"Then take it with you."

"Now?"

"When else?"

Daniel stood, opened the box, and took the bowl back out. It was lighter than he remembered.

"And the chair?" Jana asked.

Daniel looked at the list.

"Get rid of it."

"You sure?"

He heard her tone and had to smile.

"Yes."

Jana checked the box.

It was not a big decision. That was exactly why Daniel noticed how strangely final the sound of pen on paper felt.

He tucked the bowl under his arm.

He left the chair on the list.

Jana had not asked him to grieve faster.

Only not to make his pace hers by default.

Jana did not close the binder right away.

"Friday I'll send you the comparison and the realtor's proposal."

"Good."

"And if you haven't said anything by the agreed time, I decide."

Daniel was already starting to answer.

Jana raised a hand.

"Not because I don't care what you think. Because the apartment can't wait another month for a decision from you."

Daniel looked at the ceramic bowl under his arm.

"I understand."

"Don't say you understand if what you really mean is: we'll talk about it again Friday."

He had to smile briefly.

"More precisely, then: if I don't respond, you don't wait."

"Exactly."

"And if I do respond, we argue about it."

"Very likely."

Jana closed the binder.

Daniel thought about the mailbox downstairs again. He could have kept asking whether the realtor might have touched the label. Whether the super had access to the mailboxes. Whether anyone had spent longer than usual in the hallway over the past few days.

None of those questions was completely absurd.

None was strong enough, right now, to make the crooked plastic into anything more.

He did not like the comparison.

So he kept it to himself.

Later, when they left the apartment, Daniel stopped at the mailbox once more.

The plastic strip was still crooked.

He did not check the intercom again. He did not look for scratches around the lock. He did not pull out the name label to see whether anyone had changed something underneath it.

There was no reason to.

He could press it back into place with his thumb.

He did.

The plastic clicked softly.

Jana was already at the front door.

"Done?"

"Yes."

"You sure?"

Daniel looked at her.

"Go."

Then he followed Jana out onto the street.

Outside, Jana's phone buzzed.

She looked at the screen. "The realtor."

"At this hour?"

"Apparently."

She answered and started toward the car.

Daniel stood at the curb for a moment.

He did not look back at the building.

Then he walked to the subway.
