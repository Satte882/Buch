## 47

"I wouldn't stop with the employee."

Jonas had put the page on the large screen.

Daniel read the first paragraph.

The nighttime access had now been partly explained.

The maintenance window had in fact been moved forward. The employee had had a legitimate reason to be in the building early.

What remained unresolved was the second access three nights earlier. And the old contact remained what it had been: old, but real.

Jonas had not turned that into an alarm.

His proposal was broader anyway.

Daniel did not let Jonas add the open points together.

"The second nighttime access first."

Jonas opened the logs.

11:18 p.m.

Side entrance.

Eight minutes inside the building.

No maintenance order in the regular system.

"Badge unambiguous?"

"Personalized card. Technically possible to hand it over, but prohibited. No video from the corridor. The exterior camera has a maintenance gap during that period."

Berg raised his eyebrows.

Jonas immediately said, "Scheduled camera maintenance. Documented for weeks."

Daniel nodded.

No sign of manipulation.

Only an open access event that was harder to explain because of it.

"Shift schedule?"

"Person officially off. Swap not yet clarified."

Daniel pointed to the old contact.

"And that?"

Jonas opened the old note.

No meeting in some back room.

A shared club event two years earlier. Several dozen people. Later, two digital contacts whose content was no longer available.

"So real," Daniel said.

"Yes."

"And thin."

"Also yes."

The two traces did not become stronger merely because they stood beside each other.

But they did not disappear either.

"Define the scope," Lena said.

"Not just the person and his direct functional access. All employees of the three subcontractors with active access to that section of the building for fourteen days."

Berg looked at the screen.

"How many?"

"Just under one hundred twenty."

Daniel looked up.

"And what do you want to do with them?"

"Check existing contact, time, and access relationships against the relevant records. Only for that period."

Lena asked, "Individual basis for each person?"

"Their shared access to the area at risk."

"That isn't an individual basis."

"No."

Jonas said it without defending himself.

As if that were exactly the point they had to decide.

Lena took Jonas's list of nearly one hundred twenty employees.

"What happens in practice if we approve your proposal?"

Jonas answered without drama.

"Existing access, time, and relevant contact relationships are checked against the relevant records for the defined period. Hits are reviewed manually. If a potentially incriminating hit can't be explained, I'd temporarily suspend that specific access and continue checking."

"For someone against whom there was no individual basis before."

"Then there would be the hit."

"A hit produced by a review they entered only because of proximity to their workplace."

Jonas nodded.

"Yes."

Berg said, "That's exactly the question the new safeguards are there for."

Lena looked at him.

"Safeguards don't answer whether the initial group is too large."

"No. But they make the proposal reviewable."

Daniel heard both sentences.

Both could be true at the same time.

That was the danger.

Jonas had not forgotten that misattributions existed.

He had built them into his procedure.

That had not made the measure harmless.

Only more professional.

Berg asked, "And when there are hits?"

"If a hit isn't immediately plausibly explainable, temporarily suspend access until we've clarified it."

A technical-coordination employee in the room asked, "How quickly could you pull the group?"

Not: are we allowed to do that?

How quickly.

Jonas answered, "With the existing structure, a few hours."

Daniel looked at him.

Jonas noticed.

"What?"

"Why that broad?"

"Because otherwise we end up checking only the person who catches our attention first again."

Daniel said nothing.

Jonas moved to the next point.

"We know the harm is asymmetric. If one hundred nineteen people are unremarkable, nothing happens to them. If we miss the one person abusing the access, we can't undo that later."

Daniel did not know the sentence.

But he knew every part of it.

Lost time.

Incomplete certainty.

The harm of not acting.

An instrument that now existed.

Jonas did not sound like Heller.

He sounded like a good investigator who had learned from a real case.

From Daniel.

Lena asked, "What argues against it?"

Jonas answered immediately.

"Misattributions. Old contacts. Shift overlaps without significance. And we'd be bringing people into a review group solely because of their functional proximity."

"So you have the counterarguments."

"Of course."

"And you still make the proposal."

"Yes."

Berg looked at Daniel.

"What do you think?"

Daniel looked at the two nighttime access events.

One explained.

One open.

One old contact.

No acute warning.

No security guard in the hospital.

No final time window moved forward.

But also no proof there was nothing behind it.

Jonas's threat scenario was not absurd.

That made it harder.

Daniel thought of the first nighttime access.

12:44 a.m.

Sixteen minutes before the written maintenance window.

An hour earlier, it would have appeared on a list of unusual access events.

Now it was explained.

If they had broadly reviewed the one hundred twenty people before that explanation arrived, perhaps more hits like it would have appeared.

The hits would have been real, only without the context that made them ordinary.

Daniel pointed to the line they had already clarified.

"If we'd seen this access yesterday and didn't yet know about the maintenance call—how would your procedure have treated it?"

Jonas thought briefly.

"As a high-priority hit whose alternative explanation needs to be checked immediately."

"Suspend access?"

"Not automatically."

"Why not?"

"Because the plausible work explanation would be the first obvious one."

Daniel nodded.

Jonas could test the alternative explanation.

The problem was not that he had forgotten the old way of working.

The problem was where it now began.

The technical-coordination employee asked again, "So do we check the open explanation first, or run the large group in parallel?"

No one in the room behaved as though the question itself might be fundamentally out of bounds.

Daniel looked at Jonas's proposal.

Then at the clarified nighttime access.

The new starting line was visible between them.

Daniel looked again at the list of nearly one hundred twenty people.

Then at Jonas.

"What's your strongest counterhypothesis?"

Jonas did not hesitate.

"That the second access was work-related too, just documented badly. And that the old contact has no significance whatsoever for today's case."

Daniel nodded.

"What would support that hypothesis?"

Jonas glanced briefly at Lena.

Then back at the screen.

"Shift changes. Maintenance order. Shared device or badge use. And an explanation for why that contact appeared at all back then."

The technical-coordination employee asked, "Do we check that first, or in parallel?"

Daniel looked at the clock.

Then moved it out of his line of sight.

He did not say, "First."

He did not say, "In parallel."

He looked at Jonas.

"How strong is your counterhypothesis?"
